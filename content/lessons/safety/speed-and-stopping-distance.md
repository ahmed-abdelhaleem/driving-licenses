## Stopping distance = reaction distance + braking distance

When something happens ahead, the car keeps moving while you **notice** it, **decide** and **move your foot** to the brake. That distance is the **reaction distance** ([[term:reaktionsstracka|sv]]). Then the car slows down over the **braking distance** ([[term:bromsstracka|sv]]). Together they make the **stopping distance** ([[term:stoppstracka|sv]]).

## Reaction distance

Your **reaction time** ([[term:reaktionstid|sv]]) is often around **1 second** for an alert driver who is expecting something – longer if you are tired, distracted, under the influence, or surprised.

::: key A quick way to calculate
In one second you travel the speed in km/h **divided by 3.6** metres. A simple rule: take the **speed in tens and multiply by 3**.
- 50 km/h → about **14 m** per second (5 × 3 = 15 m by the rule)
- 90 km/h → about **25 m** per second (9 × 3 = 27 m)
- 110 km/h → about **31 m** per second (11 × 3 = 33 m)
:::

So at 90 km/h with a one-second reaction time, you travel about 25 metres **before the brakes even start to work**. With a two-second reaction – for example if you looked at your phone – it is 50 metres.

## Braking distance – the square of the speed

The energy of a moving vehicle grows with the **square** of the speed. Trafikverket explains it like this: crash forces increase quickly with speed, because the kinetic energy increases with the square of the speed [[src:tv-safety-facts]]. The braking distance works the same way:

- **Double the speed → four times the braking distance.**
- **Three times the speed → nine times the braking distance.**

::: example How the numbers grow
If a car needs about 15 m to brake from 50 km/h on a dry road, then from 100 km/h it needs about **60 m** (4 × 15), and from 70 km/h about **29 m** (15 × 1.4²). On a wet road the distances are longer, on snow and ice **many times** longer.
:::

These figures are only examples – the real braking distance depends on tyres, brakes, the road surface, the slope and the load.

## Why a few km/h matter so much

At the moment a driver at **50 km/h** has stopped, a driver who started at **60 km/h** at the same place – with the same reaction time and brakes – is still moving fast. The extra speed is "left over" at the end of the braking, exactly where the obstacle is.

::: warning Speed decides how hard the crash is
Trafikverket reports that for unprotected road users over 65, the risk of injury is **three times higher at 50 km/h than at 30 km/h** [[src:tv-safety-facts]]. Modern car fronts are designed to reduce injuries to pedestrians, but that only helps at impact speeds **below about 50 km/h** – above that, the forces are so great that the front's design hardly matters [[src:tv-safety-facts]].
:::

## Choosing your speed

The law requires a speed at which you can always **stop within the distance you can see** to be clear, and before any obstacle that can be foreseen [[src:tf|3:14]]. This is the practical meaning:

- In a **blind bend**, over a **hill crest**, in **fog** or **darkness** with dipped beam, your stopping distance must be shorter than the road you can see.
- On a **narrow road** where you may meet someone, you need to stop within **half** the distance you can see – the oncoming car needs the other half.
- The curriculum requires you to explain how **speed affects the risk of being killed or injured** [[src:tsfs-2011-20|3:2]].

## Following distance

Keep a distance so that you will not run into the vehicle in front if it slows down or stops [[src:tf|3:2]]. Because the car in front also needs time to brake, the gap you need is mainly your **reaction distance** plus a safety margin – which is why the gap is often counted in **seconds**, not metres:

::: steps
1. Pick a fixed point beside the road – a sign or a pole.
2. When the car in front passes it, count "one thousand and one, one thousand and two, one thousand and three".
3. If you reach the point before you have finished, you are too close.
:::

Three seconds is a common rule of thumb in good conditions – it is not a legal limit. Make the gap **at least double** on wet roads and **up to ten times** greater on icy roads [[src:uk-hc-general|rule 126]].
