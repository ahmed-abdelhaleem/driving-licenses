The risk education requires you to explain what **sleep** means, how **tiredness** affects you when you drive, how to **recognise** tiredness, and how to **avoid** its risks [[src:tsfs-2012-40|5:3]].

## What the law says

The same rule that forbids driving under the influence also covers tiredness: a vehicle may not be driven by anyone who, because of illness, **exhaustion** (*uttröttning*), alcohol or other substances, cannot drive it safely [[src:tf|3:1]].

There is no fixed "tiredness limit" like the one for alcohol. But if you **know** you are tired and drive anyway, it is a conscious risk that can lead to liability for **careless driving** (*vårdslöshet i trafik*) [[src:vti-fatigue-law]] [[src:tbl|1]].

## How common is it?

According to VTI, the Swedish road and transport research institute [[src:vti-fatigue]]:

- Tiredness may contribute to about **10–40 percent** of all crashes.
- In surveys, about **30 percent** of drivers aged **20–24** say they have **fallen asleep or nearly fallen asleep** while driving during the past year.
- The risk rises if you have **slept too little**, been **awake too long**, or drive **late at night or early in the morning**.
- Groups at special risk are **young drivers**, professional drivers, shift workers and people with **sleep disorders**.

## What tiredness does

A tired driver **reacts more slowly**, **notices less** and finds it harder to keep the vehicle steady in the lane. In the end you can **nod off** for a moment – and at 90 km/h, the vehicle travels **25 metres every second**.

Tiredness and **alcohol** make each other worse: alcohol has a dampening effect, so you do not notice the tiredness creeping in [[src:tv-alcohol-effects]].

## Warning signs

::: warning Stop when you notice these
- You **yawn** again and again, your eyes feel **heavy** or you **blink** a lot.
- You find it hard to keep your **head up**.
- You **cannot remember** the last few kilometres.
- You **miss** a sign, an exit or a change of speed limit.
- The vehicle **drifts** in the lane or onto the rumble strips, and your **speed** varies without you meaning it to.
- You start to feel **cold**, restless or irritated.
:::

The **rumble strips** (*räfflor*) milled into the centre and edge of many roads make a loud noise and vibration when you drift onto them. Studies cited by VTI show that rumble strips on the edge of 110 km/h motorways cut deaths and serious injuries in single-vehicle crashes by about **30 percent** [[src:vti-fatigue-law]]. But if they wake you, it is a sign to **stop**, not to carry on.

## When you should not drive at all

VTI recommends that you **avoid driving** if [[src:vti-fatigue-law]]:

| Situation | Limit |
|---|---|
| You have been **awake too long** | **19 hours** or more |
| You have **slept too little** | **Less than 5 hours** in your last sleep |
| The body clock is at its **lowest** | Late at night, about **03:00–06:00** |

Also avoid **long periods** of driving without breaks, especially at night. If you have a **sleep disorder** – treated or not – be careful about driving, especially when you have slept badly [[src:vti-fatigue-law]].

## When tiredness hits

::: steps
1. **Stop** as soon as you can do so safely – VTI's advice is to **stop at once** when you feel tired [[src:vti-fatigue-law]].
2. Park where it is **safe and allowed** – a rest area, a car park, a petrol station. On a motorway or expressway you may only stop at signed **parking places or rest areas** – not on the shoulder [[src:tf|9:1]] – so take the next one or the next exit.
3. **Rest or sleep** before you continue.
4. Let a **rested passenger** with a licence drive, if there is one.
:::

Opening the window, loud music or a cold fan may make you feel more alert for a short while, but they do **not** remove the tiredness. Only **rest and sleep** do.

## Plan so it does not happen

- **Sleep well** before a long trip, and do not start a long drive after a full day of work or school.
- Plan **breaks** before you need them, and share the driving.
- Avoid driving in the middle of the night when you can.
- Remember that some **medicines** make you drowsy – see [[lesson:alcohol-and-drugs]].

::: only motorcycle
**On a motorcycle,** wind, noise, cold and vibration can make you tired faster. A tired rider is slower to spot hazards and to react – and has no seat belt or car body. Take breaks more often, keep warm, and drink water.
:::
