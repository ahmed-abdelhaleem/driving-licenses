## Bends are where riders crash

Trafikverket's analyses of fatal motorcycle crashes show that the most common crash type is the **single-vehicle crash**, and the most common **place** is a **bend**. Speeds were often **above the limit** – only about **three in ten** riders were judged to have kept to the speed limit in these crashes. In **eight of ten** head-on crashes where a rider died, the **motorcycle crossed into the oncoming lane** [[src:tv-mc-safety]].

So bends are where a rider's skill, speed choice and planning matter most.

## How a motorcycle steers

At walking pace you steer by turning the handlebars the way you want to go. Above roughly jogging speed, a motorcycle steers by **leaning**, and you make it lean by **counter-steering**:

::: key Counter-steering
To turn **right**, push the **right** handlebar grip **forward** (away from you). The front wheel turns slightly **left** for an instant, the motorcycle leans **right**, and it turns right. To turn **left**, push the **left** grip. The harder you push, the faster it leans.
:::

[[fig:counter-steering]]

Counter-steering is the fastest way to change direction – it is also what you use in the **evasive manoeuvre** of the riding test, done at about 50 km/h [[src:tv-mc]].

## Speed before the bend

::: steps
1. **Read the bend early** – signs ([[sign:A1|A1]], [[sign:A2|A2]], [[sign:X1|X1]]), the line of trees, poles and fences, and how the road edge narrows into the distance.
2. **Slow down before you lean** – finish your braking while the motorcycle is **upright**. Braking hard while leaned over uses grip you need for cornering.
3. **Choose a gear** that lets you drive through the bend with **light, steady throttle**.
4. **Look through the bend** – turn your head and look as far ahead as you can see, to where you want to go. The motorcycle tends to go where you look.
5. **Lean** with a positive push on the grip and keep a **smooth, steady throttle** through the bend.
6. **Accelerate gently** out of the bend when you can see the exit.
:::

The law requires you to keep a speed at which you can stop within the distance you can see [[src:tf|3:14]] – in a blind bend that distance can be very short.

## Position and line

- In a **right-hand** bend, a position **towards the middle** of your lane gives a better view ahead – but never so close to the centre line that your head or shoulders lean into the oncoming lane.
- In a **left-hand** bend, a position **towards the right** of your lane gives a better view – but watch for gravel and the road edge.
- A "late" turn-in – waiting until you can see further round the bend – lets you see the exit before you commit, and keeps you away from the oncoming lane.

::: warning The oncoming lane
Never let the motorcycle drift towards the centre line in a left-hand bend. Oncoming vehicles – often cutting the bend themselves – and your own lean mean your head can cross the line even when the wheels do not.
:::

## What reduces your margin

- **Gravel, sand, leaves, wet road markings, manhole covers, tar repairs and diesel spills** – see [[lesson:mc-road-surface]].
- **A passenger or luggage** changes the handling and lengthens braking.
- **Cold or worn tyres** grip less.
- **Tension** – stiff arms make the motorcycle hard to steer. Relax your arms, grip the tank with your knees.

::: tip If you are going too fast for the bend
Do **not** panic-brake or straighten up. **Look** where you want to go, keep a steady throttle and **lean more** – a modern motorcycle can usually lean further than the rider believes. If you must brake, brake **progressively**, as gently as possible while leaning. Prevention – lower speed before the bend – is far better.
:::

The curriculum requires you to use the **right riding technique in different situations** and understand how the **laws of nature** affect the motorcycle [[src:tsfs-2011-19|2:2]].
